The integers are as discrete as anything in mathematics. There is no limit, no derivative and no integral in the statement "there are $25$ primes up to $100$". Yet some of the deepest facts about the integers — how many primes lie below $x$, how they split among residue classes, how often $n$ has many divisors — were first proved, and in many cases can only be proved, with calculus, complex analysis and Fourier analysis. This lecture explains why that works.

The short answer: most natural questions about the integers are not questions about one integer but about **how many** integers have a property, up to a large bound $x$. A count up to $x$ is a function of a real variable. As $x$ grows, the individual integers blur together and the count starts to behave like a smooth function — and smooth functions are what analysis is built for.

## Counting functions and asymptotics

<Definition name="Arithmetic function">
An **arithmetic function** is any function $f : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{C}$. Its **summatory function** is
$$
S_f(x) = \sum_{n \le x} f(n), \qquad x \ge 1 \text{ real}.
$$
</Definition>

Typical examples are the indicator of the primes (whose summatory function is the prime-counting function $\pi(x)$), the number of divisors $d(n)$, and the von Mangoldt function $\Lambda(n)$, which equals $\log p$ when $n$ is a power of a prime $p$ and $0$ otherwise.

An individual value $f(n)$ is often wild: $d(p) = 2$ for every prime $p$, while $d(720720) = 240$. The summatory function is much tamer. The goal is an **asymptotic formula**
$$
S_f(x) = M(x) + E(x),
$$
where the main term $M(x)$ is an explicit smooth function and the error term $E(x)$ is provably smaller. We use the standard notation:

- $f = O(g)$ or $f \ll g$: there is a constant $C$ with $|f(x)| \le C g(x)$ for all large $x$.
- $f = o(g)$: $f(x)/g(x) \to 0$; and $f \sim g$: $f(x)/g(x) \to 1$.
- $f = \Omega(g)$: $f = o(g)$ is false, so $|f(x)| \ge c\, g(x)$ for some $c > 0$ and arbitrarily large $x$.
- $\varepsilon$ denotes an arbitrarily small positive number, and implied constants may depend on it.

Much of the subject is a contest over error terms: the main term is usually easy to guess, and the work lies in proving how small $E(x)$ is — and showing it cannot be smaller.

## Why analysis applies

There are three main bridges from the discrete to the continuous. Every later lecture uses at least one of them.

### Sums become integrals

A sum of a smooth function over the integers is close to the corresponding integral. The basic tool is summation by parts, the discrete analogue of integration by parts.

<Result name="Abel summation">
Let $a(n)$ be an arithmetic function with $A(x) = \sum_{n \le x} a(n)$, and let $\phi$ be continuously differentiable on $[1, x]$. Then
$$
\sum_{n \le x} a(n)\, \phi(n) = A(x)\, \phi(x) - \int_1^x A(t)\, \phi'(t)\, dt .
$$
</Result>

Taking $a(n) = 1$, so that $A(t) = \lfloor t \rfloor = t - \{t\}$, and $\phi(t) = 1/t$ gives
$$
\sum_{n \le x} \frac{1}{n} = \log x + \gamma + O\!\left(\frac{1}{x}\right),
$$
where $\gamma = 0.5772\ldots$ is Euler's constant. The integer part $\lfloor t \rfloor$ has been split into a smooth piece $t$, which produces the main term $\log x$, and a bounded oscillating piece $\{t\}$, which produces the constant and the error. This "smooth part plus fluctuation" split recurs throughout the subject; Lecture 2 applies it to the divisor function.

### Generating functions turn multiplication into analysis

Multiplicative structure — every integer factors uniquely into primes — is encoded by **Dirichlet series**.

<Definition name="Dirichlet series">
The Dirichlet series of an arithmetic function $f$ is
$$
F(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{f(n)}{n^s}, \qquad s = \sigma + it \in \mathbb{C},
$$
wherever it converges. For $f = 1$ it is the **Riemann zeta function** $\zeta(s) = \sum_{n \ge 1} n^{-s}$, which converges absolutely for $\sigma > 1$.
</Definition>

Two features make Dirichlet series the right tool:

1. **Multiplication of series is Dirichlet convolution.** If $h(n) = \sum_{ab = n} f(a) g(b)$, then $H(s) = F(s) G(s)$. For example, $d(n) = \sum_{ab = n} 1$, so $\sum_n d(n) n^{-s} = \zeta(s)^2$.
2. **Unique factorization is an Euler product.** For $\sigma > 1$,
$$
\zeta(s) = \prod_{p} \left(1 - \frac{1}{p^s}\right)^{-1}.
$$
Expanding each factor as a geometric series and multiplying out produces every $n^{-s}$ exactly once — which is the statement that every $n$ factors uniquely into primes.

The Euler product already proves something. As $s \to 1^+$, the left side tends to infinity because the harmonic series diverges. Taking logarithms of the right side, $\log \zeta(s) = \sum_p p^{-s} + O(1)$, so $\sum_p 1/p$ must diverge. In particular there are infinitely many primes, and more: they are dense enough that their reciprocals diverge, which is false for the squares. Euler gave this argument in the eighteenth century. It is the prototype of the subject: **arithmetic information about primes is read off from the analytic behaviour of $\zeta(s)$ near a singularity.**

The bridge back from the Dirichlet series to the sum is Perron's formula, a contour integral that recovers $\sum_{n \le x} f(n)$ from $F(s)$:
$$
\sum_{n \le x} f(n) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \int_{c - i\infty}^{c + i\infty} F(s)\, \frac{x^s}{s}\, ds \qquad (c \text{ large enough},\ x \notin \mathbb{Z}).
$$
Shifting the contour to the left picks up residues at the poles of $F(s)\, x^s / s$. A pole at $s = 1$ produces a main term of size $x$; the remaining integral is the error term, and its size depends on how large $F$ is further to the left. For the primes, $F(s) = -\zeta'(s)/\zeta(s)$ has poles at the zeros of $\zeta$, so **the zeros of the zeta function control the error in counting primes**. That is why the Riemann hypothesis, a statement about where a complex function vanishes, is a statement about primes.

### Fourier analysis detects congruences

To count integers in a residue class or with an additive property, we expand the condition in a Fourier series on the finite group $\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}$:
$$
\mathbf{1}_{n \equiv a \ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)} = \frac{1}{q} \sum_{r=0}^{q-1} e\!\left(\frac{r(n - a)}{q}\right), \qquad e(\theta) = e^{2\pi i \theta}.
$$
For multiplicative questions one uses Dirichlet characters instead of additive characters, which leads to Dirichlet $L$-functions — the tool Dirichlet introduced in 1837 to prove that every residue class $a$ mod $q$ with $\gcd(a, q) = 1$ contains infinitely many primes. Additive characters lead to exponential sums $\sum e(f(n))$, whose cancellation is the engine behind most improved error terms in Lecture 2.

## The main results this leads to

Combining these bridges gives the landmark theorems of the subject.

<Result name="Prime number theorem">
As $x \to \infty$,
$$
\pi(x) \sim \frac{x}{\log x}, \qquad \text{and more precisely} \qquad \pi(x) = \operatorname{li}(x) + O\!\left(x\, e^{-c\sqrt{\log x}}\right),
$$
where $\operatorname{li}(x) = \int_2^x dt/\log t$ and $c > 0$ is a constant. It was proved independently by Hadamard and by de la Vallée Poussin in 1896, by showing that $\zeta(s)$ has no zeros on the line $\sigma = 1$.
</Result>

<Result name="Riemann hypothesis, prime form">
The Riemann hypothesis — that every non-trivial zero of $\zeta(s)$ has real part $\tfrac12$ — is equivalent to
$$
\pi(x) = \operatorname{li}(x) + O\!\left(x^{1/2 + \varepsilon}\right) \quad \text{for every } \varepsilon > 0.
$$
</Result>

<Result name="Mertens">
$$
\sum_{p \le x} \frac{1}{p} = \log \log x + M + O\!\left(\frac{1}{\log x}\right),
$$
where $M = 0.2614972\ldots$ is the Meissel–Mertens constant.
</Result>

None of these statements mentions analysis, but none has a proof that avoids estimating sums by integrals or using the analytic structure of $\zeta(s)$ in some form. (Elementary proofs of the prime number theorem exist, but they are still analytic in spirit: they are built on asymptotic estimates of sums.)

## A worked example

The table compares three discrete sums with their smooth approximations. Every entry was computed exactly.

| $x$ | $\sum_{n \le x} 1/n$ | $\log x + \gamma$ | $\sum_{p \le x} 1/p$ | $\log\log x + M$ | $\pi(x)$ | $x/\log x$ | $\operatorname{li}(x)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $10$ | $2.92897$ | $2.87980$ | $1.17619$ | $1.09553$ | $4$ | $4.34$ | $6.17$ |
| $100$ | $5.18738$ | $5.18239$ | $1.80282$ | $1.78868$ | $25$ | $21.71$ | $30.13$ |
| $10^3$ | $7.48547$ | $7.48497$ | $2.19808$ | $2.19414$ | $168$ | $144.76$ | $177.61$ |
| $10^4$ | $9.78761$ | $9.78756$ | $2.48306$ | $2.48182$ | $1229$ | $1085.74$ | $1246.14$ |
| $10^6$ | $14.392727$ | $14.392726$ | $2.88733$ | $2.88729$ | $78498$ | $72382.41$ | $78627.55$ |

(Here $\operatorname{li}(x) = \int_0^x dt/\log t$ in the principal-value sense; it differs from $\int_2^x$ by the constant $1.045\ldots$.)

<Example name="Checking the smallest row by hand">
For $x = 10$: $1 + \tfrac12 + \cdots + \tfrac{1}{10} = 7381/2520 = 2.92897$, while $\log 10 + \gamma = 2.30259 + 0.57722 = 2.87980$. The difference $0.04917$ is close to $1/(2x) = 0.05$, the next term in the expansion. The primes up to $10$ are $2, 3, 5, 7$, so $\pi(10) = 4$ and $\sum_{p \le 10} 1/p = \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac15 + \tfrac17 = 247/210 = 1.17619$.
</Example>

Three lessons can be read off the table:

- **The smooth approximation improves as $x$ grows**, in relative terms and, for the harmonic sum, in absolute terms too: its error is $1/(2x)$ to leading order, about $5 \times 10^{-7}$ at $x = 10^6$.
- **Different sums converge at very different speeds.** The harmonic error shrinks like $1/x$, the Mertens error far more slowly. That speed reflects the analytic object behind the sum: $\lfloor t \rfloor$ for the harmonic sum, the zeros of $\zeta$ for sums over primes.
- **The right main term matters.** $\pi(x) / (x/\log x)$ is $1.084$ at $x = 10^6$ and approaches $1$ only slowly, while $\pi(x)/\operatorname{li}(x) = 0.998$. Both are correct asymptotically, but $\operatorname{li}(x)$ is the main term that Perron's formula actually produces.

## Extensions

The same three bridges extend in several directions that later lectures develop:

- **Lattice-point problems.** $\sum_{n \le x} d(n)$ counts lattice points under the hyperbola $ab = x$, and $\sum_{n \le x} r_2(n)$ counts lattice points in a disc. The main term is an area, and the error term measures how lattice points fluctuate near a curved boundary — a problem in exponential sums.
- **$L$-functions.** Replacing $\zeta(s)$ by $L(s, \chi)$, or by $L$-functions attached to modular forms, carries the method to arithmetic progressions, quadratic forms and Fourier coefficients of automorphic forms.
- **Sieve methods.** When an Euler product is not available, sieves estimate counts by inclusion–exclusion with carefully truncated weights, again producing main terms and error terms.
- **The circle method.** Fourier analysis on $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ counts representations of integers as sums of primes or powers (Waring's problem, Goldbach-type problems).

Lecture 2 puts all of this to work on a single arithmetic function: the number of divisors.
